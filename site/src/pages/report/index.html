Evaluating AI integration at XJTLU

ADS401 Students’ Perspectives on AI at XJTLU

01 · Summary

Abstract

We surveyed 27 ADS401 students and interviewed seven to understand how they chose and used university-provided and external AI. Although 24 of 27 had heard of XJTLU AI, only eight had used it for ADS401 in the previous four weeks. External AI users most often selected studying course content, while interviews described using campus AI to find university information. These findings from one module suggest a starting point for XJTLU AI: help students find official course materials and verify answers against them.

02 · Overview

Introduction

XJTLU supports learning with AI tools such as Junmou AI1 and the XIPU AI Tutor. A Learning Mall teaching case reports generally favourable student feedback on a customised version of the tutor in ACC307, an XJTLU module2.

In ADS401 (Evaluation Methods and Statistics, 2026–27), we examined awareness and adoption of campus AI, usage patterns across coursework tasks, and the perceived benefits, barriers and differences students reported when choosing between campus and external AI. This formative study uses students’ accounts to identify which coursework tasks the teaching team could support and which would be useful to test in a follow-up evaluation.

We used an explanatory sequential mixed-methods design. We first ran a cross-sectional online questionnaire to examine awareness, use and perceptions. We then used semi-structured interviews to explore the reasons behind students’ choices.

What is XJTLU AI?

In our questionnaire, XJTLU AI means any student-accessible AI agent service officially provided by the university, including Junmou AI and the XJTLU Library AI Portal. External AI means independently accessed services outside XJTLU’s official offerings, such as ChatGPT, DeepSeek and Kimi.

Research questions

  1. RQ1 · Awareness and adoption. To what extent are students in our ADS401 module aware of and adopting XJTLU AI?
  2. RQ2 · Usage patterns. How do students in ADS401 use XJTLU AI for their academic tasks?
  3. RQ3 · Adoption factors and perceived comparison. What perceived benefits, barriers, and differences between XJTLU AI and external AI services are associated with students’ adoption and intentions to continue, try, or resume using XJTLU AI?

03 · Rationale

Related work and evaluation rationale

When we designed the study for Presentation 2, we wanted to understand what made XJTLU AI worth trying or using again. Davis’s Technology Acceptance Model highlighted two factors, perceived usefulness and ease of use. We adapted both into short, exploratory questions about ADS4013. Research on technology adoption and students’ use of ChatGPT also helped us consider whether access and support might affect tool choice4,5.

Farinosi and Melchior’s study helped us frame questions about when students use AI and how they feel about it. Johnston et al. offer another example of how to survey students about AI use in higher education6,7. For examples of questions about students’ experiences, usefulness and trust, we consulted Chung et al.’s Monash survey instrument8.

We asked students to rate each tool by activity, since a tool that helps with one task may be less useful for another9,10. We also asked whether students found XJTLU AI reliable and trusted its answers. Research on ChatGPT use for assessments likewise treats trust as a factor in its own right11.

Research gap

The ACC307 case describes feedback on a tutor configured for a single module. For ADS401, we needed evidence about how students perceived the broader set of XJTLU AI services and chose among campus and external tools for their own coursework.

Evaluation rationale

The survey mapped students’ reported use and let us compare ratings for the same activity among students who had used both tools. Following Ivankova et al.’s sequential design, we used the survey results to decide what to explore in interviews12. We then read the interview themes alongside the survey patterns to understand the reasons behind students’ choices13.

Questionnaire design and deployment

Our team designed the questionnaire together and published it in English and Chinese on Tally14. It asked about students’ backgrounds and recent AI use for ADS401, then used Tally’s conditional logic to show relevant follow-up questions.

Who answered which questions

Q9 placed students in one of four groups based on which types of AI they had used for ADS401 in the previous four weeks. The questionnaire routing map shows which follow-up questions each group answered.

Questionnaire pilot and task walkthrough

We planned an internal check of the questionnaire in Presentation 2. The version history shows what changed before launch. Which changes came from pilot feedback is unclear.

For the task walkthrough, a volunteer entered prompts while a member of our research team took notes. The volunteer asked the Learning Mall AI Tutor about presentation requirements, XIPU AI about the syllabus and LibAI about past papers. The observation log records the answers, sign-in steps, errors and delays.

Why we used an exploratory design

For RQ1 and RQ2, we first needed to map how ADS401 students used AI for coursework before comparing tools. A controlled experiment would have required fixing tasks and success criteria in advance. This exploratory formative design establishes real coursework patterns so future studies can select representative tasks and measures.

Research ethics

We kept interview contact details separate from the survey analysis. The report uses survey totals and paraphrases from the interviews.

05 · Analytical approach

Data preparation and analysis

Preparing the survey and interview data

We used ChatGPT and Python to process responses from 27 students (23 in English and 4 in Chinese). We matched the questions and response options across the two forms so we could analyse the responses together.

We coded interviews with 7 students and grouped 25 codes into seven themes15. We read those themes alongside the survey results to understand why students tried a tool or how they checked its advice. Some interview examples took place before the survey’s four-week window. We also grouped the eight problems from our walkthrough by where each occurred.

How we compared the tools in Presentation 3

We excluded N/A from rating summaries. For each activity, we paired usefulness ratings only when the same student rated both tools (Q12 and Q21), then subtracted the external AI rating from the XJTLU AI rating. Overall preference was a separate question, and we retained “Unable to compare” as a response option.

6.1 · RQ1 · Awareness and adoption

High awareness, limited recent adoption

A cross-sectional snapshot of 27 respondents. Awareness, ever-use, recent use and frequency are separate states recorded at one time, not a tracked conversion funnel.

Students broadly know XJTLU AI (24 of 27), but only 8 used it for ADS401 in the past four weeks, and only 2 of those 8 used it five or more times.

6.2 · RQ2 · Usage patterns

Different tools, different activities

Five activity categories, multiple-select. XJTLU AI users = G1 + G4 (n = 8); external AI users = G1 + G2 (n = 22). Percentages use each tool’s own users.

XJTLU AI is reported mainly for campus resources (8 of 8); external AI is used across learning tasks, led by in-course content (13 of 22).

6.3 · RQ3 · Experience, barriers, comparison and intentions

Neutral experience, external preference, weak continuation

Four lenses on RQ3: how recent users rated XJTLU AI (n = 8), why external-only users avoided it (n = 15), how dual users compared the tools (n = 7), and whether recent users intended to continue.

Perceived experience

Recent users rated six statements on a 1–5 scale; each item has seven valid ratings and one N/A.

For every item, at least 5 of the 7 valid ratings were neutral or negative; efficiency and ease of use for ADS401 tasks had median 2, and the other four items median 3.

Barriers and overall preference

External-only users selected up to three reasons for non-use; dual users chose which tool worked better overall.

Preference for external AI (9 of 15) and unclear capabilities (6 of 15) lead the barriers; 4 of 7 dual users preferred external AI overall.

Matched comparison

Five dual users rated both tools for finding campus resources; each row is one participant’s pair.

The five matched pairs are mixed (2 ties, 2 higher for XJTLU AI, 1 higher for external AI); the median difference is 0, so they cannot rank the tools.

Intention to continue

Recent users were asked how likely they were to continue using XJTLU AI for a suitable ADS401 task in the next four weeks (Q16, n = 8).

Only 1 of 8 recent users was likely to continue using XJTLU AI, and 5 of 8 were unlikely or very unlikely.

6.5 What we saw in the walkthrough

In the walkthrough, the volunteer tried two AI agent services provided by XJTLU. LibAI returned search and sign-in steps for finding past papers, while Junmou AI (XIPU AI) could not confirm the current ADS401 syllabus. Both examples point to the value of clearer links to official university and course materials.

07 · Interpretation

Discussion, limitations and recommendations

What the results suggest

Among the 22 recent external AI users, 13 selected in-course content. In interviews, students described using familiar external tools to understand concepts and plan assignments, and using XJTLU AI to find university information. Our walkthrough showed what that help could look like: LibAI gave the volunteer steps for finding past papers, while Junmou AI (XIPU AI) could not confirm the current syllabus. These examples suggest a practical role for campus AI in directing students to official resources. The five paired ratings for finding campus resources were mixed, leaving relative usefulness uncertain.

The ACC307 case reported generally favourable feedback on its customised tutor2. In ADS401, recent users’ ratings of XJTLU AI were mostly neutral or negative. ACC307 asked about a tutor configured specifically for Advanced Taxation, and our survey asked about XJTLU AI services more broadly. The different tools and survey questions limit what we can infer from the two sets of responses.

Limitations

  • One module. We heard from volunteers in ADS401. Their experiences may differ from those of other ADS401 students or students in other modules.
  • Self-reported use. We asked students what they remembered doing and what they thought of the tools. AI literacy and reliance would require their own measures16,17.
  • Interview coverage. We interviewed volunteers from G1 and G2 only. We had no interviews with students who used neither type of AI (G3) or only XJTLU AI (G4).
  • One point in time. We asked about recent use and future intentions. We would need follow-up data to see whether students kept using the tools.
  • Informal comparison. The survey and interviews capture perceptions, as in Henderson et al.’s comparison of student views on AI and teacher feedback18. The eight logged problems came from a walkthrough with one volunteer and one researcher. A standard comparison would need the same tasks, timing and scoring for each tool.

Recommendations by research question

We drew these recommendations from the Presentation 3 findings.

  1. RQ1 · Make XJTLU AI easier to find and get started with

    Teaching team and developers. Use the same XJTLU AI name and description across campus services, with one clearly labelled entry point on the Learning Mall course page. Show staff and students what the service can do and how to get started with a course task.

  2. RQ2 · Connect XJTLU AI to course and academic resources

    Lecturers and module leader. Link the module handbook, lecture materials and approved revision resources from the course page. The module leader can work with developers to make those resources searchable through XJTLU AI, giving students a reason to use it for coursework.

    XJTLU AI developers. Connect the assistant to library collections and other academic resources. Link users to the original material while respecting course and library access permissions.

  3. RQ3 · Make answers easier to check and problems easier to resolve

    XJTLU AI developers. Explain what the assistant can do19. Link course answers to the source material and show where to look when the assistant cannot retrieve it.

    ADS401 teaching team. Share course tasks that students found difficult. Test those tasks with developers to see where users get stuck, lose time or receive answers they cannot verify.

Follow-up evaluation

At the outset, we lacked the formative evidence needed to choose representative ADS401 tasks and fair criteria for comparing tools. We therefore used questionnaires, interviews and a walkthrough to establish what students used AI for and where they needed help. A follow-up experiment could ask students to complete two tasks with each tool:

  1. Find the current ADS401 module handbook.
  2. Check a statistical explanation against official course material.

Use the same instructions and scoring criteria for both tools. Record whether students find a verifiable source, reach the correct answer and complete the task, and how long they take.

Appendix D · Exploratory task observation

The walkthrough surfaced problems with finding current course information, reaching university resources and recovering from interaction errors. We assigned one primary code to each of the eight logged issues. The walkthrough captured selected attempts, so these codes describe the kinds of problems we observed.

Walkthrough records

  1. Record 01 · The Learning Mall AI Tutor returned an error

    The volunteer asked the module’s AI Tutor to list the presentation requirements by topic. Both attempts returned “Something went wrong: There was an error processing your request. Try again later.”

  2. Record 02 · Junmou AI misidentified the module’s focus

    The prompt referred to ADS401 as “Evaluation and Algorithms”. Junmou AI described it as a course in data science and AI, discussing algorithms and model assessment. The module focuses on evaluation methods.

  3. Record 03 · Junmou AI could not confirm the current syllabus

    When asked for the current syllabus, Junmou AI gave a generic outline and suggested checking Learning Mall, the module handbook and the assessment brief.

  4. Record 04 · LibAI gave directions for finding past papers

    Both times the volunteer asked for ADS401 past papers, LibAI suggested signing in to the university portal, selecting Past Exam Papers and searching for ADS401. LibAI said the papers required the student’s own login and returned no papers.

  5. Record 05 · Junmou AI gave a speculative course description

    Asked to explain ADS401 to an HCI postgraduate, Junmou AI said it could not confirm the official course content. It labelled the description it offered as an unofficial guess.

  6. Record 06 · XIPU AI models gave different answers to the same prompt

    Some models found information about ADS401. One model reviewed 10 webpages and reported finding no public official module outline or syllabus.

  7. Record 07 · An assistant reply was missing from chat history

    The chat history showed the user’s prompt and timestamp. The assistant’s reply was missing, and the interface offered no error message or recovery option.

  8. Record 08 · LibAI had no usable results after 10 minutes

    LibAI reached step 18 while searching for ADS401 study materials and was still running. After 10 minutes, it had returned no usable results.
